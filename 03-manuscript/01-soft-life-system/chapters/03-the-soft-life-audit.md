# Chapter 3: The Soft Life Audit

You cannot build a lighter business until you know what's weighing it down.

Most solopreneurs skip this step. They want the solution — the template, the tool, the system — and they want it now. But skipping the audit is like renovating a house without checking the foundation. You might make it prettier, but you won't make it safer.

The Soft Life Audit is a four-part review of where your business is actually costing you: **Time, Money, Energy, and Joy**. It takes about an hour to complete, and it will change the decisions you make for the next ninety days.

Before we build anything, we're going to measure.

---

## Why Measurement Feels Uncomfortable (and Why It's Worth It)

There's a reason we avoid auditing our lives. Measurement makes the invisible visible. And once you see the truth, you can't unsee it.

You might discover that your most profitable-sounding client is actually costing you money. You might realize that the offer you've been protecting is the one draining your energy. You might find out that you're working sixty hours a week but only twelve of them are Genius work.

That can feel bad. But it's also the beginning of freedom.

Here's the deal I make with every client before an audit: **You are not allowed to judge yourself. You are only allowed to observe.** The audit is not a report card. It's a map. It shows you where you are so you can choose where to go next.

So take a breath. Get curious. Put on your researcher hat. We're just collecting data.

---

## Part 1: The Time Audit

The first audit is the most concrete: where do your hours actually go?

For seven days, track your time. Don't try to be perfect. Use a notebook, a spreadsheet, or a time-tracking app. The goal is not precision; it's pattern recognition.

At the end of the week, sort your hours into these categories:

1. **Genius work** — the work only you can do.
2. **Growth work** — marketing, sales, relationships, learning.
3. **Delivery** — fulfilling the work clients pay for.
4. **Admin & operations** — invoices, scheduling, email, tech, filing.
5. **Firefighting** — urgent problems, mistakes, unexpected requests.
6. **Meetings & calls** — client calls, discovery calls, internal calls.
7. **Content & marketing production** — writing, recording, designing, posting.
8. **"Work about work"** — planning, organizing, deciding, researching tools.

Then calculate two numbers:

- **Total hours worked:** ___
- **Hours spent on Genius + Growth work:** ___

Divide the second by the first. That's your **Leverage Ratio**.

If your Leverage Ratio is below 30%, you're spending most of your time on work that doesn't grow the business or require your genius. That's your starting point. No judgment — just data.

**The key question:** If you could remove half of your Grunt work and firefighting, what would you do with the time?

Write the answer down. That's your first draft of the soft life.

---

## Part 2: The Money Audit

The second audit is about money — not just how much you make, but how much each part of your business actually earns.

For each offer or client, calculate:

1. **Revenue:** How much did this bring in last month/quarter?
2. **Hours:** How many hours did it take, including admin and communication?
3. **Revenue per hour:** Revenue ÷ Hours.
4. **Stress cost:** On a scale of 1–10, how much energy did it drain?
5. **Joy cost:** On a scale of 1–10, how much did you enjoy it?
6. **True profitability:** Revenue minus direct costs (tools, contractors, materials).

Now sort your offers by **revenue per hour**, not total revenue. This is the number that matters for a soft life.

You will likely find a pattern:

- One or two offers earn the most per hour.
- One or two offers look profitable but are actually expensive when you count your time.
- One or two offers drain you so much that even if they pay well, they're not worth it.

This is the moment of truth. The offers at the top of your revenue-per-hour list are the ones to double down on. The ones at the bottom are candidates for repricing, productizing, or retiring.

**The key question:** Which offer would you keep if you could only keep one?

Circle it. That's the seed of your One-Offer Business.

---

## Part 3: The Energy Audit

The third audit is about energy — the currency nobody puts on a spreadsheet.

For one week, rate each activity you do on two dimensions:

- **Energy given** (1–10): Did this fill you up or drain you?
- **Value created** (1–10): Did this move the business forward?

Plot each activity on a simple grid:

| | Low Value | High Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High Energy** | Hobbies, learning, some creative work | **Your zone of genius** |
| **Low Energy** | Pure drain | Necessary but draining |

The top-right quadrant is your genius zone. That's where you want to spend more time.

The bottom-left quadrant is pure drain. That's where you want to eliminate, automate, or delegate first.

The top-left and bottom-right are the interesting ones. Top-left activities might be things you love but that don't pay — good for life, not for business. Bottom-right activities are valuable but draining — candidates for systems and support.

**The key question:** What are the three activities that drain you most while creating the least value?

Those are your first targets for transfer.

---

## Part 4: The Joy Audit

The fourth audit is the one most business books skip: joy.

A soft life is not just a life with more time and money. It's a life that feels good. So we need to ask: what do you actually want?

Take a piece of paper and answer these questions without editing yourself:

1. What did you love about your business when you started?
2. What do you dread every week?
3. What would you do with ten extra hours a week?
4. What kind of work makes you lose track of time?
5. What kind of client lights you up?
6. What kind of client drains you?
7. What does a good day look like for you?
8. What does a good week look like?
9. What are you pretending not to know about your business?
10. If money were handled, what would you spend your days doing?

Your answers are the design brief for the business. They tell you what to protect and what to release.

**The key question:** What is one thing you would stop doing tomorrow if you were brave enough?

Write it down. You might just do it.

## The Soft Life Scorecard

Now let's bring it all together. Give yourself a score from 1 to 10 on each dimension:

| Dimension | Question | Score (1–10) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Time** | Do I control how I spend my working hours? | |
| **Money** | Does my income come from high-leverage work? | |
| **Energy** | Do I finish most days with energy left? | |
| **Joy** | Do I enjoy the work I do most days? | |
| **Boundaries** | Can I stop working without guilt? | |
| **Systems** | Does my business run on documented processes? | |
| **Leverage** | Could my business survive without me for two weeks? | |
| **Vision** | Do I know what I'm building toward? | |

Add up your scores. The maximum is 80.

- **60–80:** You have a strong foundation. Focus on optimization and maintenance.
- **40–59:** You have pockets of strength but significant leaks. Prioritize the SOFT Method.
- **20–39:** Your business is heavily dependent on you. Start with simplification and one offer.
- **Below 20:** You're in survival mode. Be gentle with yourself. Focus on the smallest possible next step.

Your score is not a verdict. It's a starting line.

## What the Audit Usually Reveals

After doing this with hundreds of solopreneurs, I've noticed the same patterns come up again and again:

**Pattern 1: The busy trap.**
Most people work more hours than they think, and most of those extra hours are Grunt work. The business feels busy, but the leverage is low.

**Pattern 2: The profitable illusion.**
The offer that looks most profitable is often the one that costs the most in time and energy. Revenue per hour tells a different story than revenue alone.

**Pattern 3: The energy mismatch.**
People spend their best hours on their least meaningful work. They give their genius to email and their leftovers to their craft.

**Pattern 4: The joy gap.**
Many solopreneurs have built a business that looks successful but doesn't feel good. They've optimized for money and forgotten to optimize for life.

**Pattern 5: The clarity gap.**
Most people cannot explain what they do in one sentence, which means their clients can't either. That's a marketing problem, a pricing problem, and a confidence problem all at once.

If you see yourself in these patterns, good. You're normal. And you're exactly where you need to be to start.

## How to Read Your Audit

When you finish the four audits, you'll have a lot of numbers. Don't let them overwhelm you. The goal is not to analyze every data point. The goal is to notice patterns.

Here's how to read your audit:

**Look for the biggest gap.**
Where is the distance between what you want and what you're doing the largest? Maybe you want fifteen hours but you're working fifty. Maybe you want to feel energized but you're drained by Wednesday. Maybe you want to enjoy your money but you're anxious about every invoice. The biggest gap is your first priority.

**Look for the biggest leak.**
Which category is consuming the most time or energy without producing proportional value? For most solopreneurs, it's admin, communication, or firefighting. The leak is where the leverage is.

**Look for the biggest surprise.**
What did you learn that you didn't expect? Maybe you thought you spent most of your time on client work, but the audit shows you spend most of it on marketing. Maybe you thought you loved a certain offer, but the joy audit reveals it drains you. Surprises are clues.

**Look for the pattern, not the outlier.**
One bad week doesn't define your business. Look at the trends across the week. Are there consistent drains? Consistent joys? Consistent gaps? The pattern is the truth.

**Look for what you're avoiding.**
The audit often reveals the thing you've been avoiding: the offer you need to retire, the client you need to fire, the boundary you need to set, the system you need to build. The avoidance is usually a signal. It's pointing at the change you know you need to make.

You don't need to fix everything the audit reveals. You need to see it clearly, choose one thing, and start there.

## The Common Mistake: Auditing With Shame

The biggest mistake people make with the Soft Life Audit is turning it into a weapon against themselves.

They look at the numbers and think: *I'm so disorganized. I'm wasting so much time. I should be further along. What's wrong with me?*

Shame is not a useful audit tool. It doesn't produce clarity. It produces paralysis.

The audit is not a report card. It's a map. It's not here to tell you whether you're good or bad. It's here to show you where you are, so you can decide where to go.

When you audit with curiosity instead of judgment, something shifts. You stop asking "What's wrong with me?" and start asking "What's happening here?" You stop seeing your time as evidence of your worth and start seeing it as information you can use.

This matters because the soft life is not built on shame. It's built on self-respect. You can't create a life that feels good by beating yourself up for the life you have now.

So as you review your audit, notice the shame voice. Thank it for trying to protect you. Then set it aside. You're not here to judge the past. You're here to design the future.

## The One Decision

The audit is not just information. It's a decision-making tool. By the end, you should be able to answer one question:

**What is the single biggest source of unnecessary weight in my business right now?**

Is it too many offers? A draining client? A lack of systems? Poor pricing? No boundaries? A business model that requires your constant presence?

Write it down. Circle it.

That's your first domino. We're going to knock it over.
