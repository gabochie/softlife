# If you want to own the business

Some readers will finish this guide and realise the problem is not the job. It
is the employment model. If you want to own the business, the career data in
this guide is still useful, but it answers a different question.

BLS data tells you what employees earn in an occupation. It does not tell you
what a solo consultant, coach, designer, or therapist earns after expenses.
Those numbers depend on pricing, utilisation, marketing, and how much of the
work you can sell before you deliver it.

## The honest test

A business is a better fit than a job if:

- You have a skill people already pay for.
- You can describe the outcome in one sentence.
- You can reach buyers without a large ad budget.
- You can deliver the work without burning out.
- You have enough savings to cover a slow start.

A business is a worse fit if:

- You need predictable income immediately.
- You are using the business to escape a boundary you have not set as an
  employee.
- You are relying on one platform, one client, or one viral moment.
- You cannot yet price the work without discounting.

## The math that matters

For a service business, the number that matters is not the median salary. It is:

```
take-home = (hourly rate × billable hours) − expenses − tax
```

A $100/hour rate with 10 billable hours a week is not a $100,000 business. It
is roughly $1,000 a week before expenses, tax, and unpaid admin. That is why
the “high-ticket” fantasy so often collapses.

## The bridge from career to business

The safest transition is not a leap. It is a bridge:

1. Keep the job or reduce hours.
2. Sell one offer to three paying clients.
3. Document the delivery process.
4. Raise the price before adding more clients.
5. Leave when the business covers your enough number for three consecutive
   months.

This is slower than the internet version. It is also less likely to force you
back into a worse job.
