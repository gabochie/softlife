# What the research got wrong

The original career research was not useless. It identified the right tension:
women are looking for work that pays enough without taking the whole day, and
the most attractive paths are often the least stable. The problem is the salary
math.

Viral career lists mix three different numbers:

1. What a top performer reports.
2. What a course creator needs you to believe is possible.
3. What a median worker actually earns.

When those numbers are collapsed into one range, the range sounds authoritative
and becomes impossible to check. This chapter checks it.

## The three patterns

**Pattern 1: Tech claims usually survive; wellness and beauty claims usually
do not.** Data scientist, cybersecurity, technical writer, UX/UI, project
management, and actuary are broadly supported. Exercise trainer, skincare
specialist, travel agent, and medical coder are not. The difference is not
that wellness work is bad. It is that the market pays a median wage, and the
content economy sells the top decile.

**Pattern 2: The lower bound is often a mid-career number.** A $90,000 floor for
technical writing is above the BLS 10th percentile ($57,440). A $100,000 floor
for project management is essentially the median ($102,320). These numbers can
be real for someone with experience. They are not entry numbers.

**Pattern 3: The highest-ceiling roles are missing from the fantasy.** Actuary,
optometrist, and information security analyst have high medians and high top-end
pay, but they require exams, doctoral study, or years of specialist experience.
The soft-life version of these roles is real. It is just not fast.

## What we removed

We removed medical coding from the 24-role list because the source claimed
$90,000–$100,000, while the BLS median for medical records specialists is
$51,140. The occupation is projected to grow 7.8 percent and has 14,000 annual
openings, so it may still be a reasonable local choice. It is not a six-figure
soft-life shortcut.

We also did not include “customer success manager,” “revenue cycle analyst,” or
“brand strategist” as standalone roles because BLS does not track those titles
cleanly. That does not mean they are bad jobs. It means a national salary claim
would be invented.

## How to use a salary claim

When you see a career salary range, ask:

- What is the exact occupation, not the job title?
- Is this mean, median, or a selected sample?
- Is it national, state, or city?
- Does it include commission, equity, or bonuses?
- Is it full-time, part-time, or freelance?
- What is the 10th percentile?
- What is the entry path and who pays for it?

If the source cannot answer those questions, treat the number as marketing, not
data.
