# Sources, method, and next step

## Source notes

**Pay.** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage
Statistics, May 2025. Figures are annual medians for full-time wage and salary
workers unless otherwise stated. The 10th and 90th percentiles are from the
same occupation and reference period.

**Growth and openings.** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Employment
Projections, 2025–35. Annual openings include growth and replacements.

**Occupation matching.** Each role is matched to a BLS Standard Occupational
Classification (SOC) code. Where the source research used a job title that BLS
does not track, the closest occupational match is named and the mismatch is
noted.

**Telework.** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Population Survey, 2025
annual averages, table 60. The 2025 figures are 11-month averages that exclude
October 2025 because of the federal government shutdown. Role-level telework
is a group proxy, not a role-specific measurement.

**Access.** Direct BLS pages blocked automated requests during retrieval.
CareerFacts.org was used to retrieve the BLS OEWS and projection figures.
Every role profile includes the SOC code so the figure can be checked against
the original BLS tables.

## The Soft Life Score

```
income score   = min(100, median pay ÷ 150,000 × 100)
schedule score = schedule control (1–5) × 20
energy score   = energy (1–5) × 20
entry score    = entry ease (1–5) × 20
growth score   = clamp(50 + growth % × 2.5, 0, 100)
openings score = min(100, annual openings ÷ 50,000 × 100)
demand score   = 0.6 × growth score + 0.4 × openings score

Soft Life Score =
  0.30 × income
+ 0.25 × schedule
+ 0.20 × energy
+ 0.10 × entry
+ 0.15 × demand
```

Schedule, energy, and entry are editorial scores. They are not BLS data. The
weights are a judgement about what makes work sustainable, not a scientific
claim.

## Limitations

- National figures cannot tell you what a role pays in your city.
- Medians hide the difference between junior, senior, freelance, and
  part-time work.
- Projections are not guarantees. They can be wrong.
- The 24 roles are a curated set, not a representative sample of the labour
  market.
- The editorial scores reflect one team's judgement. Change them for your life.
- The guide does not provide legal, immigration, tax, or financial advice.

## Ten questions for an informational interview

1. What does an ordinary Tuesday look like in this role?
2. Which parts of the work are predictable, and which are reactive?
3. How much control do you have over your schedule and location?
4. What does the entry path look like for someone without a traditional
   background?
5. What did you spend on training, licensing, or portfolio building?
6. What is the realistic income in year one, not year five?
7. What is the biggest hidden cost of this work?
8. What makes people leave this role?
9. What would you do differently if you started again?
10. Who else should I speak to, and may I use your name?

## The 30-day check-in

At the end of your first test month, answer these five questions:

- Did the work give me energy or take it?
- Did the pay math hold up against my enough number?
- Did the entry path fit my real constraints?
- Did the people doing the work describe a life I want?
- What evidence do I have that this is more than a fantasy?

If you cannot answer with evidence, do another test. Do not confuse more
research with progress.

## If you only do one thing

Book one conversation with someone doing the work. Ask about the ordinary week,
not the highlight reel. Then write down what surprised you.

That single conversation is worth more than another ten career lists.
